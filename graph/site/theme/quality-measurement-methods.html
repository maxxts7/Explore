Automatic overlap scores versus human and preference judgments

Deciding, with a number, that one model's answer beats another's turns out to need two entirely different kinds of instrument. This theme covers both: automatic overlap scores like ROUGE-L and BLEU, which compare text against a fixed reference, and judgment-based metrics like win rate and Elo score, which ask a human or a model to simply prefer one output over another.

The two families sit in direct contrast here, and inter-labeler agreement runs underneath both, since every judgment-based number is only as trustworthy as the humans producing it.

Both InstructGPT and Constitutional AI need to say, quantitatively, whether one model's output is better than another's, and this theme gathers the two families of measurement the corpus uses to do that. Automatic overlap metrics compare generated text against a reference: ROUGE-L for summarization, BLEU for translation, and F1 score for span-overlap question answering, all cheap to compute but blind to whether an answer is actually preferred. Judgment-based metrics instead ask an evaluator to compare or rate outputs directly: InstructGPT's win rate reports how often labelers prefer one model's output over a fixed baseline, and its Likert quality rating scores a single output on a 1-7 scale; Constitutional AI's Elo score performs the analogous role from crowdworker pairwise comparisons, while its absolute harmfulness score rates a single conversation on a 0-4 scale via a model trained on crowdworker ratings. Inter-labeler agreement rate underlies all of the judgment-based measures, since a preference metric is only as trustworthy as the consistency of the humans producing it.

Members — a guided walk

  1. ROUGE-L

    ROUGE-L opens this theme as the cheapest available yardstick: an n-gram/longest-common-subsequence overlap score that grades a generated summary against a reference summary on CNN/DM and Reddit TL;DR without needing a human in the loop.

  2. BLEU

    BLEU applies the same reference-overlap logic to a different generation task, scoring WMT 2015 French-to-English translations; like ROUGE-L it is fast and reproducible but only measures surface overlap with one fixed reference, not whether an answer is actually preferred.

  3. F1 score (span-overlap QA metric)

    F1 score extends the overlap-metric family to extractive QA on DROP, QuAC, and SQuADv2. Its sharpest role in this theme is negative: it diverges under RLHF from win-rate, collapsing on span-overlap QA even as win-rate climbs, exposing exactly what a reference-overlap score cannot see about preference.

  4. Win rate (preference against baseline)

    Win rate is the metric reference-overlap scores cannot substitute for: it directly asks labelers whether they prefer one model's output over a fixed 175B SFT baseline, capturing the human judgment that ROUGE-L, BLEU, and F1 abstract away, becoming InstructGPT's headline number precisely because of that gap.

  5. Likert overall-quality rating

    The 1-7 Likert overall-quality rating corroborates win-rate by scoring a single output on an absolute scale, patching win rate's structural weakness of being anchored entirely to one fixed baseline whose choice could rescale every reported number.

  6. Elo Score (helpfulness/harmlessness)

    Elo score generalizes win-rate's underlying logic: instead of comparing every model to one designated anchor, it aggregates pairwise preferences across many models and snapshots into a single running rating, which Constitutional AI needs since it tracks helpfulness and harmlessness across a whole training trajectory.

  7. Absolute Harmfulness Score

    Absolute harmfulness score cross-checks Elo score's central limitation: Elo can only rank models against each other, not say whether any of them is safe in absolute terms, and its gaps can shift merely because crowdworker instructions changed, so a model trained on absolute-harm ratings supplies the missing reading.

  8. Inter-labeler agreement rate

    Inter-labeler agreement rate closes the theme by naming the assumption every judgment-based measure above has rested on: a win rate, Likert score, Elo rating, or harmfulness score is only as trustworthy as the consistency of the humans who produced it, and it explicitly caps the resolution of win rate itself.
