Three levers on one model: prompting, finetuning, steering

A chat model's behavior can already be pushed around before generation even starts, by writing a better instruction into the system prompt, or well before that, by finetuning the weights themselves. So a fair question about any new steering method is not just whether it works, but whether it earns its place next to tools people already reach for. This theme covers what happens when the paper puts activation steering directly up against both.

The page walks through both comparisons: steering layered on top of positive and negative system prompts, where each lever adds control the other alone does not reach, and steering set against finetuning the model on the same data, where steering matches or beats finetuning on several behaviors and generalizes better to open-ended writing. It closes on the practical difference behind both results, that building a steering vector takes a few forward passes and minutes, where finetuning needs a real training run.

Contrastive Activation Addition is not offered as a replacement for the two standard levers on a chat model's behavior; the paper tests it against both and finds it composes with each. System-prompting, a custom instruction prepended to the context that Llama 2 Chat is trained to adapt to, is the first lever, chosen over few-shot prompting, which supplies prior examples of the desired behavior instead of an instruction and which the authors' preliminary experiments found less effective at steering the tested behaviors. The system-prompt comparison layers CAA on top of positive and negative system prompts and finds it can shift behavior beyond what either prompt alone achieves, and the reverse holds too: system-prompting adds control on top of a fixed steering multiplier. Supervised finetuning, training the model's weights directly on the same contrastive multiple-choice dataset used to build steering vectors, is the second lever. The finetuning comparison finds CAA can steer beyond finetuning's effect for three of seven behaviors and, more strikingly, generalizes to open-ended generation where finetuning sometimes cannot: for Sycophancy, finetuning on multiple-choice data fails to transfer to the free-text setting, while CAA transfers in every case. The computational efficiency comparison explains part of why steering is attractive as a lever of its own: generating a CAA vector needs only forward passes and takes under five minutes on one GPU, against roughly ten minutes on two GPUs to finetune the same model on a thousand examples.

Members — a guided walk

  1. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA)

    Contrastive Activation Addition is not offered as a replacement for the two standard levers on a chat model's behavior. The paper instead tests it against both prompting and finetuning in turn, checking not just whether CAA works on its own but whether it composes with techniques practitioners already use -- adding controllable behavior change on top of, rather than instead of, the existing toolkit.

  2. System-prompting

    System-prompting is the first lever: controlling a model's responses with a custom instruction prepended to the context, a mechanism Llama 2 Chat is specifically trained to adapt to. It is the paper's chosen prompting baseline for comparison against activation steering, selected over an alternative prompting method the authors tried and set aside.

  3. Few-shot prompting

    Few-shot prompting, supplying prior examples of the desired behavior in context instead of an instruction, was the alternative baseline considered and set aside: the authors' preliminary experiments found it less effective than system-prompting at steering the behaviors they test, so system-prompting alone carries the prompting comparison forward.

  4. CAA combined with system-prompting comparison

    The system-prompt comparison layers CAA on top of positive and negative system prompts and finds it can shift behavior beyond what either prompt alone achieves -- and the reverse holds too, since system-prompting adds further control on top of a fixed steering multiplier. Neither lever saturates the other; combined, they move behavior further than either does alone.

  5. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

    Supervised finetuning is the second lever, and a heavier one: rather than adjusting activations or context, it trains the model's weights directly on the same contrastive multiple-choice dataset used to build steering vectors, for one epoch with SGD at a learning rate of 1e-4. This reaches high accuracy, often above 90 percent, on the held-out test questions used elsewhere to evaluate steering.

  6. CAA combined with supervised finetuning comparison

    The finetuning comparison finds CAA can steer beyond finetuning's effect for three of seven behaviors, and more strikingly, generalizes to open-ended generation where finetuning sometimes cannot: for Sycophancy, finetuning on multiple-choice data fails to transfer to the free-text setting, while CAA transfers in every case -- evidence that steering existing representations generalizes better than retraining the whole model on a narrow format.

  7. CAA vs. finetuning computational cost comparison

    The computational efficiency comparison explains part of why steering is attractive as a lever in its own right, separate from how well it performs: generating a CAA vector needs only forward passes and takes under five minutes on one GPU, against roughly ten minutes on two GPUs to finetune the same model on a thousand examples.
