Limiting potential influence instead of measuring realized change

An agent does not have to change anything to become dangerous; quietly acquiring the power to change everything can be enough. This theme covers a second approach to side effects in Concrete Problems (2016), one that measures potential influence rather than realized harm, borrowing empowerment from information theory to formalize what that potential even means.

The page separates this from baseline-distance penalties before turning to reward uncertainty, a companion idea that manufactures caution not through an explicit penalty but by simply refusing to let the agent be sure what it is being rewarded for.

A second, distinct strategy for avoiding negative side effects does not measure distance from a baseline state at all, and this theme covers it. Penalize influence discourages an agent from putting itself in a position of high potential power over its environment, not merely from acting on that power once acquired, a distinction that matters because an agent could accumulate influence and simply hold it in reserve. The measure behind this proposal is empowerment, an information-theoretic quantity defined as the maximum mutual information between an agent's future actions and future states, borrowed to formalize what potential influence means. Reward uncertainty takes a different tack again, replacing a single fixed reward function with a probability distribution over rewards biased toward assuming unplanned environmental change is bad, so caution emerges from uncertainty rather than an explicit penalty term. Low-impact agents, an informally discussed prior concept, names the general target all of these proposals aim at. This theme's claim is that influence and uncertainty offer an alternative vocabulary to baseline-distance for the same underlying problem.

Members — a guided walk

  1. Low-impact agents

    Armstrong's informally-discussed prior concept of low-impact agents names the general target this whole theme aims at: agents designed not to alter their environment more than their task requires. It is usually read as a claim about realized change, only later reinterpreted here as a claim about potential change instead.

  2. Empowerment

    Empowerment supplies the formal measure for that reinterpretation: an information-theoretic quantity defined as the maximum mutual information between an agent's future actions and future states. Originally developed as an intrinsic reward to be maximized, so an agent seeks out capability for its own sake, it gets repurposed here for the opposite end.

  3. Penalize influence

    Penalize influence reinterprets low-impact agents as a claim about potential power rather than realized change (Low-impact agents — gets reinterpreted as potential power by → Penalize influence), and it takes empowerment, ordinarily maximized, and minimizes it instead (Empowerment — is minimized instead of maximized by → Penalize influence). The result discourages an agent from merely positioning itself for high influence, not just from acting on influence once acquired.

  4. Reward uncertainty (side-effect mitigation)

    Reward uncertainty trades penalty for uncertainty relative to penalize influence (Reward uncertainty (side-effect mitigation) — trades penalty for uncertainty relative to → Penalize influence): instead of hand-tuning an explicit cost for acquired capability, it replaces the fixed reward function with a distribution biased toward assuming unplanned change is bad. Caution here emerges from what the agent doesn't know about its reward, closing this theme's alternative vocabulary.
