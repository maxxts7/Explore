Interpretability itself becomes a benchmark

Judging whether a learned feature is interpretable used to mean a person squinting at examples and making a call. Here two language models do that job instead, one writing an explanation and the other testing whether the explanation actually predicts behavior.

This theme covers that scoring pipeline and the checks built to keep it honest: which text fragments get graded, whether the comparison against simpler baselines is handicapped fairly, and why a feature's statistical shape, how spiky its activations are, turns out to predict how interpretable it scores.

The paper's headline comparison rests on language models grading language-model features. In the autointerpretability score, adapted from Bills et al. 2023, GPT-4 is shown text fragments where a dictionary feature activates and writes a natural-language explanation; GPT-3.5 then simulates the feature by predicting its per-token activations from that explanation alone, and the correlation between predicted and true activations is the score (GPT-3.5 stands in for GPT-4 as simulator only because its API exposed the logprobs the protocol needs). Three controls keep that score honest. Top-and-random vs. random-only interpretability scoring names the choice of which text fragments to grade on: mixing highly-activating fragments with random ones is the default, rerun with random fragments alone in Appendix C.2 to confirm the ranking holds. The top-K-active baseline control restricts PCA and ICA to the same number of simultaneously active directions as the SAE and finds this explains only a small fraction of its advantage. And the interpretability-vs-kurtosis/skew correlation analysis finds interpretability correlates weakly, 0.19 and 0.24, with a feature's skew and kurtosis, explaining why ICA, which directly maximizes non-Gaussianity, is the strongest non-SAE baseline.

Members — a guided walk

  1. Autointerpretability score

    The autointerpretability score, adapted from Bills et al. (2023), is the paper's primary measure of whether a dictionary feature means anything: a language model is shown text where the feature activates, writes a natural-language explanation of it, and a second language-model pass predicts the feature's per-token activations from that explanation alone. The correlation between predicted and true activations is the score, and Cunningham et al. compute it across thousands of features and four baselines. Two different language models carry out the two halves of that pipeline, starting with the one that writes the explanation.

  2. GPT-4

    GPT-4 is the explainer: given a dictionary feature's highly-activating text fragments and their rescaled per-token activations, it writes the natural-language description of what the feature detects, such as 'instances of the letter W and words beginning with w' for one layer-1 residual-stream feature. Table 1 in the paper shows five such GPT-4-written descriptions alongside their scores, ranging from -0.11 to 0.57 for the first five features in that dictionary. GPT-4's explanation is only half the pipeline; a second model has to test whether that explanation actually predicts the feature's behavior.

  3. GPT-3.5

    GPT-3.5 is that second model, the simulator: given GPT-4's explanation, it predicts the feature's per-token activations on held-out text, and those predictions are correlated against the ground truth to produce the score. Cunningham et al. substitute GPT-3.5 for GPT-4 at this step, departing from Bills et al.'s original protocol, purely because OpenAI's public GPT-3.5 API exposed the logprobs the simulation step needs while its GPT-4 API did not at the time. Which text fragments get shown to these two models is itself a protocol choice with consequences for the score.

  4. Top-and-random vs. random-only interpretability scoring

    Top-and-random vs. random-only interpretability scoring names that choice: the default 'top-and-random' scoring mixes highly-activating fragments with randomly sampled ones, since with only 640 tokens of context a purely random sample would rarely contain a highly-activating example for anything but the most common features. Appendix C.2 reruns the whole comparison using stricter random-only scoring and finds the learned dictionary features still outperform every baseline, especially in early layers, ruling out top-scoring as an artifact explaining the paper's results. A second, independent control checks a different possible artifact, this time in how the baselines themselves are set up.

  5. Top-K-active baseline control

    The top-K-active baseline control, run in Appendix G, addresses an asymmetry: a PCA direction is active across an entire half-space, while a sparse-coding feature activates only past a learned bias threshold, so the PCA and ICA baselines get restricted to a fixed number K of simultaneously active directions per datapoint, set equal to the SAE's own average active-feature count at R=1 and alpha=8.6e-4. This handicap explains only a small fraction of the interpretability gap between the learned dictionary and its baselines, meaning sparsity of activation alone does not account for the advantage. What does help explain both the size of that advantage and which baseline comes closest to closing it is a property of the activation distributions themselves.

  6. Interpretability-vs-kurtosis/skew correlation analysis

    The interpretability-vs-kurtosis/skew correlation analysis correlates a feature's autointerpretability score with statistical moments of its activation distribution, finding weak positive correlations of 0.19 with skew and 0.24 with kurtosis. Heavier-tailed features, in other words, tend to score as more interpretable, an intuitive fit with the idea that interference from other active features should be roughly normally distributed by the central limit theorem, leaving genuine features to stand out as outliers. This is also the analysis that explains ICA's position among the four baselines: because ICA directly maximizes non-Gaussianity, it lands closest to the SAE's scores of any baseline tested, closing the loop between what this pipeline measures and why some methods score better on it than others.
