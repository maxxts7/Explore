Chapter 1The thesis: neurons hide more than they show (5 concepts)

The paper's central claim, stated in its abstract and defended for the rest of its length, is that polysemanticity — individual neurons activating in multiple, semantically distinct contexts — is caused by superposition, models representing more features than they have neurons by assigning them to an overcomplete set of directions rather than to individual neurons. Sparse autoencoders are offered as a scalable, unsupervised method for recovering those directions directly, aimed at providing “a foundation for future mechanistic interpretability work.”

One tradeoff behind every design choice in the method theme (11 concepts)

Nearly every design choice in Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) is a position on one tradeoff: minimize a Reconstruction loss plus a Sparsity loss (L1 penalty on feature activations) weighted by a coefficient alpha, with the Dictionary expansion factor (R) setting how overcomplete the hidden layer is and Tied encoder/decoder weights halving the parameter count by forcing encoder and decoder to share values. Reconstruction never reaches zero — swapping in Pythia-70M's reconstructed layer-2 activations raises Pile perplexity from 25 to 40 — and sweeping alpha traces a smooth Sparsity-reconstruction tradeoff (no single correct decomposition) with no distinguished “knee,” weak evidence against there being one uniquely correct decomposition.

Chapter 2Two routes to audit: elicit what you suspect, or enumerate everything (4 sections)

Validating that training has made a model robustly safe is hard, because unwanted behavior that survives training can still be hard to find. CAA's steering paper and this paper propose two different routes to that audit, both left as an ambition rather than a finished result — CAA's a future-work paragraph, this paper's a closing conclusion, but structurally quite different in what they can find.

Elicit a suspected behavior, or enumerate every feature theme (4 concepts)

CAA's route is elicitation: push a model's activations toward an unwanted behavior deliberately, on the premise that a behavior easily elicited by a small internal perturbation is plausibly reachable by some input in deployment too, so failing to elicit it is a stronger guarantee than failing to find it by sampling outputs. This paper proposes a second, structural route instead: Enumerative safety, the aspiration that a complete, human-understandable list of a model's features could itself guarantee it will not perform dangerous behaviors — audit not by eliciting behavior but by enumerating and inspecting the parts that could produce it.

Chapter 4An unsupervised alternative, reopened by a 2025 paper (4 sections)

Sparse autoencoders and steering vectors are two different answers to the same question — how do you find a direction in activation space that means something — and it takes Persona Vectors, in 2025, to state the contrast directly. From this paper's own 2023 standpoint the comparison did not exist yet; a steering vector, in the sense the later papers mean it, is a concept this paper's dictionaries predate.

The advantage a 2025 paper names in its own limitations edge

A steering vector, whether CAA's Mean Difference or Persona Vectors' automated pipeline, requires picking a target concept in advance and building labeled contrastive examples of it before any direction can be computed. This paper's autoencoder needs neither — trained on unlabelled activations alone, it discovers thousands of directions with no concept specified up front. Persona Vectors' own limitations section names that unsupervised property a genuine advantage over its own supervised pipeline: SAEs “may... enable unsupervised discovery of persona-relevant directions, including specific traits that cannot be easily elicited through prompting.”

Reading a direction out, versus writing one in connective theme (10 edges)

A sparse autoencoder's dictionary features are learned to read a direction's presence out of the residual stream, an unsupervised decomposition with no target concept specified in advance; a steering vector is built to write a chosen direction into the residual stream, always starting from a labelled concept and contrastive examples. Persona Vectors is the paper that states this pairing directly, describing sparse autoencoders as finding directions “in an unsupervised way.” Reading out and writing in are the same underlying move, projecting onto or adding along a direction in the same vector space, run toward opposite ends.

Figure 1 — Sample, train, interpret: the SAE pipeline

This diagram lays out the paper's three-stage method: sample a language model's internal activations (residual stream, MLP, or attention sublayer) while it runs over a text corpus; train a sparse autoencoder with tied encoder and decoder weights and a ReLU bottleneck to reconstruct those activations as a sparse combination of learned directions; then interpret the resulting dictionary features, for instance by scoring them with an automated interpretability procedure. Dictionary features score higher on automated interpretability than baselines

This experiment asks whether the learned dictionary features are individually more interpretable than directions found by neurons, PCA, ICA, and random baselines, using an automated procedure that has a language model describe a feature from its activating text and then predict its activation elsewhere. Table 1 and Figure 2 give the headline comparison, and the remaining figures and Table 2 in this group stress-test that result against dictionary size, scoring method, the MLP sublayer, and possible statistical confounds.

Table 1 — Layer-1 feature descriptions and interpretability scores

Five dictionary features from the layer-1 residual stream, picked in the arbitrary order they appear in the dictionary, each paired with a GPT-4-written description and its autointerpretability score. It gives a concrete, worked example of what the automated interpretation pipeline outputs before the paper moves to aggregate comparisons, ranging from a fairly clean 'letter W' feature to a barely interpretable 'actions' feature.

Figure 2 — Interpretability score by layer: sparse coding vs. baselines

This chart compares the mean autointerpretability score of the learned dictionary features against four baselines (ICA, PCA, random directions, and the untransformed neuron basis) across residual-stream layers 0 through 5. It is the paper's headline result: dictionary features score noticeably higher in early layers, though the gap narrows through the model and becomes comparable to ICA by the later layers shown.

Figure 8 — Interpretability by layer across seven dictionary sizes

This figure repeats the interpretability-by-layer comparison for seven different dictionary-size ratios, from half the model's dimension up to 32 times overcomplete. It shows that the interpretability advantage of sparse coding is not limited to large, overcomplete dictionaries but also holds for smaller ones, which just explain less of the overall activation variance.

This plot recomputes interpretability scores using only randomly sampled text fragments, dropping the top-activating fragments that make scoring easier. It addresses the worry that mixing high-activation examples with random ones inflates the scores, and finds that although absolute scores drop for every method, dictionary features still outperform the baselines, especially in early layers.

This pair of plots repeats the top-and-random and random-only interpretability comparisons for the MLP sublayer instead of the residual stream. It shows the approach transfers to the MLP with mixed success: many features are still more interpretable than neurons, but the paper notes elsewhere that MLP dictionaries also produce a large number of features that never activate.

Table 2 — Interpretability correlated with activation moments

This table reports the correlation between a feature's interpretability score and four statistical moments of its activation distribution: mean, variance, skew, and kurtosis. Skew and kurtosis show the strongest positive correlations, supporting the idea that interpretable features have heavy-tailed activations, which also explains why ICA, which maximizes non-Gaussianity, is the strongest of the baseline methods.

This chart adds fixed-K variants of ICA and PCA as further baselines, matching the number of directions active per token to the average number of active dictionary features. It checks whether the sparse coding advantage in Figure 2 is just an artifact of comparing directions active over an unequal share of activation space, and finds this accounts for only a small part of the difference.

Dictionary features localize a causal behavior with fewer, smaller edits than PCA

Using activation patching on the indirect object identification task, this experiment tests whether editing the model along dictionary-feature directions can reproduce a target behavior with fewer interventions and smaller edits than editing along PCA directions. Figure 3 gives the main result on one layer of Pythia-410M and Figure 17 repeats the same measurement across five additional layers.

Figure 3 — IOI patching efficiency: dictionary features vs. PCA

These two plots track KL divergence from a target output as more features are patched (left) and as edit magnitude grows (right), comparing sparse dictionary features to PCA components and to a non-sparse dictionary on layer 11 of Pythia-410M. Dictionary features reach a given KL divergence with fewer patches and smaller edits than PCA, and that advantage disappears for the non-sparse dictionary, indicating sparsity itself drives the improvement.

Figure 17 — IOI feature-patching precision across six more Pythia-410M layers

This figure reruns the same two KL-divergence measurements from Figure 3 across six residual-stream layers of Pythia-410M (layers 3, 7, 11, 15, 19, and 23) instead of just layer 11. It shows the causal-localization advantage of dictionary features over PCA is not specific to one layer but holds fairly consistently across the depth of the model.

Individual features are monosemantic, predictable, and chainable into circuits

This set of case studies zooms into a handful of hand-picked dictionary features to check three properties of a genuine feature: it fires on a single human-understandable input pattern, it has a sensible effect on output logits when removed, and its activation can be traced back through features in earlier layers. Figures 4, 11 through 15 build and stress-test the input and output story around an apostrophe feature, while Figure 5 assembles a multi-layer causal tree for a closing-parenthesis feature.

Figure 4 — Token histograms for the apostrophe feature (feature 556)

This pair of histograms shows which tokens activate dictionary feature 556 (left) and which output tokens lose the most predicted probability when the feature is ablated (right). The feature fires almost exclusively on apostrophes, and removing it most strongly suppresses the model's prediction of a following 's', matching how apostrophes precede 's' in possessives and contractions like 'let's'.

Figure 5 — Causal-tree circuit for the closing-parenthesis feature

This causal tree traces a layer-5 feature that fires on tokens inside parentheses and predicts a closing parenthesis, tracking backward through four earlier layers via ablation experiments to find which upstream features feed it. The resulting tree of interpretable upstream features, such as ones detecting acronyms or tokens right after an open parenthesis, demonstrates that circuit detection can be automated using dictionary features instead of individual neurons.

Figure 11 — Default-basis dimension resembling the apostrophe feature

This histogram shows token activations for the default-basis residual-stream dimension that most resembles the apostrophe feature from Figure 4. Unlike that clean dictionary feature, this raw dimension only clusters around apostrophes at its highest activations and represents unrelated information at lower activations, illustrating the polysemanticity that sparse coding is meant to remove.

Figure 12 — 'If'-in-code feature: input and output histograms

This pair of histograms shows the input activations and output-logit effects of a dictionary feature that fires on the word 'if' specifically in coding-like contexts. It is one of several worked examples used to show that individually interpretable, narrowly-scoped features recur across the learned dictionary, not just for the apostrophe case.

Figure 13 — 'Dis'-prefix feature: input and output histograms

This pair of histograms documents a feature that fires on the 'Dis' prefix as part of specific word bigrams, such as 'disCLAIM', 'disclosed', and 'disordered'. It shows that some dictionary features track sub-word token patterns rather than whole semantic concepts, broadening the kinds of monosemantic structure the method can recover.

This pair of histograms shows a second apostrophe-related feature, one that activates specifically in 'I'll'-like contraction contexts rather than apostrophes generally. Alongside Figure 15, it shows the model uses several distinct apostrophe features tied to different families of contractions rather than one catch-all apostrophe feature.
